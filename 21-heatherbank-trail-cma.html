Section 09 · Option 2 · Reverse mortgage

Stay in the home, take equity out tax-free, and repay from the eventual sale.

A reverse mortgage lets homeowners aged 55 and over borrow against the home with no monthly payments. Interest is added to the balance and compounds, and the loan is repaid with interest when the home is sold, when the owners move out permanently, or from the estate. Title stays with the owners, the lender guarantees the balance can never exceed the home's value, and because the advance is a loan rather than income, it is not taxed. Two federally regulated banks lead the market, HomeEquity Bank (CHIP) and Equitable Bank, with Home Trust and Bloom as alternatives.

Who qualifiesAll owners on title aged 55 or over. The amount is set by the younger owner's age, the appraisal and the location.
How muchUp to 55% of appraised value (Equitable up to 59%). See the age table below.
Rates todayCHIP 5-year fixed 6.94% as of August 31, 2026, up from 6.39%. Equitable 3-year fixed 5.99% and 1-year 4.99% on a promotion to September 30. Rates reset to the lender's rate at the end of each term.
Set-up costsClosing or administration fee of $995–$1,795; appraisal about $300–$400; independent legal advice and legal fees about $1,000–$1,800.
Ongoing obligationsProperty taxes, insurance and maintenance must be kept current. Letting them lapse can trigger repayment.
Repayment triggersSale of the home; a permanent move, including long-term care beyond 12 months; the death of the last owner; default on taxes or insurance.
PrepaymentPermitted, with penalties inside a term. Repayment on sale is penalty-free in most contracts.
Non-resident ownersLenders require the home to be the borrowers' principal residence and expect them to live in it. An extended absence, generally more than six months in a year, can be treated as a permanent move and trigger repayment. As the owners are non-residents who are in Canada only part of the year, approval is not assured and the terms of any absence need to be settled with the lender before Option 2 is relied on.

Lender details from HomeEquity Bank and Equitable Bank published rates and fee schedules, September 2026. SYNERGY does not arrange reverse mortgages; amounts and rates are illustrative and subject to appraisal and lender approval.

What favours staying

No moveThe family keeps a home it has owned for 23 years, on a street where homes of this size rarely come up, and avoids the disruption of relocating.
Cash without a saleThe advance is tax-free, requires no monthly payments, and leaves full ownership and any future rise in value with the family.
Timing stays in the family's handsThe home can still be sold later, on the family's own schedule, with the loan and interest repaid from that sale.

The drawbacks

Cost of borrowingInterest compounds with nothing paid down. At 6.5%, a $620,000 advance grows to $1,175,000 in ten years and $1,618,000 in fifteen. The interest alone exceeds the original advance after about eleven years.
Rate riskThe rate is fixed only for the term (one to five years) and resets to the lender's rate after that. Reverse mortgage rates sit well above conventional mortgage rates; CHIP's five-year rate rose from 6.39% to 6.94% this year.
Shrinking equityInterest comes out of the family's share of the home. If the market is flat, the equity behind a $620,000 advance would be used up in about fifteen years; a rising market slows that.
Repaid from the homeThe loan and the accumulated interest are repaid when the home is eventually sold or from the estate, so less is available at that point for the next home or for the family.
Conditions on the homeTaxes, insurance and upkeep must stay current; the home must remain the borrowers' principal residence; an extended absence can trigger repayment. Prepayment inside a term carries a penalty.
EstateWhoever inherits the home inherits the balance with it. Heirs must refinance or sell, usually within a set period, to repay the lender.
Interest not deductibleBecause the money is used personally, the interest cannot be deducted for tax, unlike interest on funds borrowed to invest.
The amount is not guaranteedThe advance is set by the lender's appraisal, not by this analysis, and by the younger owner's age. A lower appraisal or a younger owner reduces it, and monthly draws stop once the approved limit is reached.

Section 10 · Option 2 · The numbers, two ways to draw

A lump sum, or a smaller advance with a monthly draw.

A reverse mortgage can be taken as a single advance, or as a small initial advance followed by scheduled monthly deposits (HomeEquity Bank's Income Advantage and Equitable Bank's Flex both offer this). Interest is only charged on what has actually been advanced, so the monthly structure costs far less over time when the money is needed for regular expenses rather than one large purpose. Both scenarios below use the same 6.5% rate, compounded semi-annually, with no repayments and the home rising 2% a year. Scenario A takes $620,000 at the outset. Scenario B takes $20,000 up front and $3,000 a month after that.

Reading the two scenarios

Over the same ten years, the lump sum delivers $620,000 on day one and accumulates about $555,000 in interest. The monthly structure delivers $380,000 spread across the decade and accumulates about $161,000, leaving roughly $635,000 more equity in the home. The lump sum suits a single large need; the monthly draw suits day-to-day living costs. In both cases the interest is paid from the home's value when it is eventually sold, so the choice between them comes down to whether the family needs a large sum now or a steady amount over time.

In short

Selling converts the full equity to cash now, at today's value, and ends the family's exposure to this house; the trade-off is the move, and giving up any future rise in value. A reverse mortgage keeps the family at Heatherbank and releases tax-free cash, at a real cost: about $555,000 of interest over ten years on a lump sum, or about $161,000 on the monthly draw, paid from the home's value when it is eventually sold. Which is right depends on how long the family intends to stay, how much cash it needs and when, and the lender's position on non-resident owners. On the numbers alone, selling gives the family the most value with the least uncertainty; the case for staying rests on how much the home itself matters and how long the family expects to be in it. Those are the questions to work through together.
